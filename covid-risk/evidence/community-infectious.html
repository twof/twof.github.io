COVID event risk calculator

Evidence and methodology for model inputs

Community infectious fraction

Default: Always fetched live from San Francisco hospital admission data. The calculator requires a successful fetch before any result tables are rendered; if the SF DataSF API is unavailable, an error is shown and the user is offered a retry. There is no static fallback — conditions change week to week and a stale fallback would mislead.

How it affects the model

This parameter represents the probability that a random person in the community is currently infectious with SARS-CoV-2 (capable of transmitting). It is used to estimate the expected number of infectious attendees at the event. A higher community prevalence means more infectious sources and proportionally higher risk.

The model uses a low and high estimate to produce a range of outcomes, reflecting uncertainty in the infection-hospitalization ratio (IHR) — the dominant epistemic uncertainty when back-calculating prevalence from hospital admissions.

Data source: SF hospital admissions

The calculator pulls live data from the San Francisco Department of Public Health, which publishes weekly COVID-19 hospital admissions for SF residents admitted to SF hospitals. Data is sourced from the CDC’s National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) via the California Department of Public Health and updates weekly on Wednesdays with a one-week lag.

Back-calculation method

Point prevalence is derived from the hospitalization rate using the infection-hospitalization ratio (IHR):

prevalence = (weekly_hosp_rate / IHR) × (infectious_days / 7) / 100,000
  • Weekly hospitalization rate: 4-week rolling average of COVID-19 admissions per 100,000 SF residents, from the DataSF API. A 4-week window is responsive to recent trends without being dominated by single-week noise.
  • Infectious period: 7 days. Omicron-era culturable virus shedding peaks in days 1–5 and is generally undetectable by day 8–10 (Boucau et al. 2022).
  • IHR range: 1.3%–1.9%. A lower IHR implies more infections per hospitalization, yielding a higher prevalence estimate. See derivation below.

Bounds from IHR range

The displayed low/high bounds reflect the IHR range applied to the 4-week average admission rate:

  • Low prevalence bound: 4-week average admission rate divided by the high IHR (1.9%).
  • High prevalence bound: 4-week average admission rate divided by the low IHR (1.3%).

Poisson sampling noise in the weekly count is real but does not materially change the decision the calculator is meant to support: the dominant uncertainty is in the IHR itself, not in how many admissions occurred.

Why hospitalization-based back-calculation?

Hospitalizations are the most consistently reported COVID metric. Unlike case counts (which depend on who tests and reports) or wastewater (which lacks a reliable site-specific prevalence conversion factor), every COVID hospitalization is captured in the NHSN reporting system.

Evidence

No direct prevalence measurement exists for SF

The gold standard for measuring community prevalence is random population sampling with PCR testing, as the UK’s Office for National Statistics COVID-19 Infection Survey (ONS CIS) did through March 2023. No equivalent survey exists for the US at any geographic level, and the ONS CIS has been discontinued.

Wastewater surveillance (WastewaterSCAN, CDC NWSS) provides directional trends but cannot be reliably converted to absolute prevalence. Multiple studies have attempted wastewater-to-prevalence calibration:

  • Mohring et al. (2024) calibrated PMMoV-normalized wastewater against a 14,000-person cohort in Germany, finding a scaling factor of γ = 0.208 ± 0.031. However, this factor is site-specific and not transferable across sewersheds.
  • Morvan et al. (2022) estimated prevalence from 45 UK wastewater sites to within ±1.1% of ONS estimates, but required site-specific covariates (flow rate, suspended solids, etc.).
  • Li et al. (2024) showed the wastewater-prevalence relationship “varies both in space and in time” and requires a hierarchical Bayesian model with national prevalence survey data as input.
  • Rainey et al. (2022) validated a mass balance equation in Gainesville, FL achieving 1.28% MAE, but the dominant uncertainty is the fecal shedding rate, which varies by 2–3 orders of magnitude across individuals.

No SF-specific wastewater-to-prevalence calibration has been published.

IHR estimates

The infection-hospitalization ratio is the key uncertain epistemic parameter. CDC burden estimates provide an implicit overall IHR:

SeasonIllnessesHospitalizationsImplied IHR
2024–202514.1–20.7M390–550K~2.7%
2025–2026 (current)3.7–11.9M110–240K~2.0–3.0%

These IHRs are for symptomatic illnesses. A substantial fraction of SARS-CoV-2 infections are fully asymptomatic (and still contribute to transmission but are not counted in the CDC illness estimates). To convert to an all-infection IHR, we divide by (1 − asymptomatic fraction).

Asymptomatic fraction

Two key sources bound the Omicron-era asymptomatic fraction:

StudyDesignAsymptomatic %Notes
Ma et al. (2022) Meta-analysis, 8 studies, n=7,640 32.4% (95% CI: 25.3–39.5%) Early Omicron (BA.1); 35.9% in populations with ≥80% vax coverage. High heterogeneity (I² = 97.7%).
Choi et al. (2025) Mathematical model, South Korea national data 49.7% (vaccinated), 44.7% (unvaccinated) Omicron era; progressive increase in asymptomatic fraction from wild-type → Delta → Omicron.

With near-universal hybrid immunity in 2026, the higher estimate (~50%) is likely more representative of current conditions. The model uses a 30–50% range to derive the IHR bounds below.

Deriving the all-infection IHR

Applying the asymptomatic adjustment to the CDC symptomatic IHR (using the current 2025–2026 lower bound of ~2.0%):

30%Ma et al. (lower bound)2.7% × 0.68 = 1.84%

The model uses 1.3%–1.9%, which directly spans the range derived from these two sources.

Limitations

  • Small-number noise. At current SF rates (~2 admissions/week), week-to-week variation is large in relative terms. The 4-week average dampens this but does not eliminate it; users should treat the displayed point estimate as a rolling smoothed value rather than a precise current reading.
  • IHR uncertainty. The all-infection IHR depends on age distribution, vaccination rates, variant, and evolving population immunity. The 1.3–1.9% range is derived from the CDC symptomatic IHR adjusted by published asymptomatic fractions, but both inputs carry uncertainty. In 2026, with continued immune buildup, the lower bound may be more representative.
  • Hospitalization captures only a subset. Not all infections lead to hospitalization, and the IHR multiplier attempts to account for this. Changes in clinical practice (e.g., treating more patients as outpatients) would affect the ratio without changing true prevalence.
  • Data lag. SF hospitalization data updates weekly with a 1-week lag, so the estimate reflects conditions ~1–2 weeks ago. The 4-week averaging window further smooths recent changes.
  • This is the most uncertain input in the model. Unlike physical parameters (room volume, ventilation rates) that can be measured, community prevalence is fundamentally unobservable and changes week to week. Users should treat these estimates as order-of-magnitude guidance.
