Evidence and methodology for model inputs

Breathing rate (m³/hr)

Default value: 0.5 m³/hr

How it affects the model

Breathing rate is a simple linear multiplier on inhaled dose: dose = concentration × breathing_rate × duration. Doubling the breathing rate doubles the inhaled dose and approximately doubles the infection probability (at low probabilities).

This parameter represents the susceptible person’s inhalation rate — how much air they breathe in per hour. It is separate from the emission side (quanta emission rates for breathing, speaking, singing), which controls how much virus the infectious person puts into the air.

Evidence

Reference values by activity level

Adult inhalation rates are well-established from respiratory physiology, the EPA Exposure Factors Handbook (2011), and the ICRP Publication 66/119 reference values:

Values used in COVID airborne transmission models

Recent COVID-19 airborne transmission studies use inhalation rates consistent with our default:

  • Aganovic et al. (2023): 0.52 m³/h — “the inhalation rate for resting and standing averaged”
  • Alsved et al. (2023): 0.54 m³/h (9 L/min) for their indoor air transmission model
  • Aganovic & Kadric (2024): 0.52 m³/h for their dose-response model comparison using human coronavirus challenge data

One outlier: Peng et al. (2022) fitted B0 = 0.288 m³/h to real outbreak data — roughly half the physiological resting rate. This illustrates that the “breathing rate” in a Wells-Riley model is an effective parameter that is coupled to the quanta emission rate. Different models can use different combinations of breathing rate and quanta rate that produce similar infection probabilities. What matters is the product of all the parameters, not any single value in isolation.

Is the default of 0.5 reasonable?

For a seated, mostly sedentary event (conference, concert, theater, religious service), 0.5 m³/h is appropriate. It sits at the upper end of the EPA sedentary range and is consistent with the values used in the three COVID transmission models cited above.

For physically active events (fitness classes, dancing, sports), users should increase this value. A dance event might warrant 1.0–1.5 m³/h; vigorous exercise could be 2.0+ m³/h. Higher inhalation rates increase both the amount of virus inhaled by susceptible attendees and (in reality, though not captured by this model) the amount of virus exhaled by infectious attendees.

Limitations

  • Single value for all attendees. The model uses one breathing rate for all susceptible attendees. In practice, breathing rate varies with age, sex, body size, and individual activity. Males breathe ~10–15% more than females at the same activity level; older adults may breathe less. This variation is small relative to the activity-level differences.
  • Inhalation rate is constant regardless of attendee activity. The model has activity-segmented emission rates (quanta/hr for breathing, speaking, singing) but uses a single inhalation rate. In reality, someone who is singing or speaking also breathes more deeply. This is a minor effect for the susceptible person (who is mostly listening) but could matter at events where everyone participates vocally.
  • Not all inhaled air reaches the lower respiratory tract. The Wells-Riley “breathing rate” is an effective parameter, not purely the physiological minute ventilation. Regional deposition in the nasal passages, pharynx, and upper airways means that only a fraction of inhaled aerosol reaches the alveolar region where infection occurs. This may partly explain why Peng et al.’s fitted value (0.288 m³/h) was lower than the physiological rate.
