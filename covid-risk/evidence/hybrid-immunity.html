COVID event risk calculator

Evidence and methodology for model inputs

Hybrid immunity

Defaults: Non-IC: 50% dose reduction. IC: 20% dose reduction.

How it affects the model

The hybrid immunity parameter represents accumulated immune protection from prior SARS-CoV-2 infections and historical vaccination. It is applied as a multiplicative reduction on the effective inhaled dose:

effective_dose = base_dose × (1 − hybrid_immunity) × …

A value of 0.50 means the effective dose is halved; a naive person with no prior exposure would have hybrid_immunity = 0.

This parameter is separate from the current-season vaccine effectiveness (ve_infection), which captures the fast-waning, variant-specific protection from the most recent vaccine dose and is applied on the source side (reducing effective prevalence via reduced shedding). Hybrid immunity captures the slower-waning protection from memory T cells, B cells, and mucosal immunity accumulated over multiple exposures.

Evidence: non-IC (50%)

By 2026, the vast majority of the US population has hybrid immunity from multiple prior infections and vaccinations. The question is how much residual protection this provides against infection with current circulating variants.

Meta-analysis of hybrid immunity against Omicron reinfection

Ma et al. (2024) pooled 17 studies through July 2024, comparing hybrid immunity to other immune states against Omicron reinfection:

US real-world effectiveness

Florea et al. (2025) studied US adults from September 2022 through July 2023 during the Omicron-dominant period:

  • Hybrid immunity VE against infection: 62.2% (95% CI: 46.0–74.5%) within 7–179 days
  • Waning to 39.4% (95% CI: 12.5–61.6%) at ≥180 days
  • Vaccination alone (bivalent): 37.2% at <60 days, declining to 21.1% at 60–179 days

Canadian population cohort

Carazo et al. (2024) analyzed 5 million residents across the BA.1 through BQ/XBB periods. Hybrid immunity with 5 doses maintained >90% effectiveness against severe outcomes through the BA.4/BA.5 period, declining to 63–91% during BQ/XBB depending on recency.

Healthcare worker cohort

Jacobson et al. (2024) followed vaccinated healthcare personnel over 13 months. Those without hybrid immunity had a 3.0× higher hazard of infection (41.7% infected vs 16.2% with hybrid immunity).

Rationale for 50%

Hybrid immunity effectiveness against Omicron infection ranges from ~65% shortly after the last immune event to ~35–40% at 6+ months. For a 2026 population where most individuals had their last immune-boosting event (infection or vaccination) several months ago, 50% is a reasonable central estimate. This translates to a 50% reduction in effective inhaled dose in the Wells-Riley model.

Evidence: IC (20%)

Immunocompromised individuals mount weaker immune responses to both infection and vaccination, resulting in less durable protection.

IC breakthrough infection risk vs general population

Bytyci et al. (2024) systematically reviewed breakthrough infection risk in vaccinated IC patients vs the general vaccinated population:

IC conditionBreakthrough RR vs non-IC
Cancer1.4×
HIV1.9×
Chronic kidney disease2.3×
Immunodeficiency2.6×
Organ transplant6.9×

Excluding the extreme transplant category, the blended RR across most IC conditions is roughly 2×.

Faster waning in IC populations

Sefik et al. (2024) found that immunocompromised children showed significantly faster decline in T cell and memory B cell responses compared to healthy peers. Hybrid immunity in IC children dropped below protective thresholds by month 12, while healthy children maintained adequate protection.

After two vaccine doses alone, IC children “failed to produce T cell and memory B cell responses reaching thresholds of protection,” requiring a 3-dose primary series and yearly or twice-yearly boosters.

Kidney transplant: hybrid immunity still helps

Stand et al. (2024) found that among kidney transplant recipients, hybrid immunity was associated with a 72% lower risk of infection (aHR 0.28) and 96% lower risk of hospitalization (aHR 0.04) compared to vaccination alone. Even in the most immunosuppressed populations, prior infection adds meaningful protection.

Rationale for 20%

The model’s IC dose factor (1 − 0.20 = 0.80) divided by the non-IC dose factor (1 − 0.50 = 0.50) yields a ratio of 1.6×, which sits within the 1.4–2.6× RR range for most IC conditions (excluding transplant). A 20% dose reduction reflects meaningfully impaired but not absent immune memory — consistent with the finding that IC individuals retain some hybrid immunity but lose it faster and mount weaker initial responses.

Limitations

  • Single value for all IC conditions. The model uses one hybrid immunity value for all IC attendees, but breakthrough RR ranges from 1.4× (cancer) to 6.9× (transplant). Severe IC conditions like recent transplant may have near-zero hybrid immunity. The IC severity mix (severe/moderate/mild) partially captures this variation through outcome rates, but not through dose reduction.
  • No time dependence. Hybrid immunity is applied as a constant, but real protection wanes over months. The model’s separate VE waning curve captures some of this for the most recent vaccine dose, but accumulated immunity from prior infections also wanes (roughly halving every 6–12 months for infection protection).
  • Variant mismatch. Hybrid immunity effectiveness depends heavily on antigenic distance between prior exposures and the currently circulating variant. A major antigenic shift could substantially reduce hybrid immunity effectiveness. The values here reflect 2024–2025 Omicron-lineage data.
  • Dose-reduction vs effectiveness. The model applies hybrid immunity as a dose-reduction factor in the Wells-Riley exponential model. At low doses (low prevalence), dose-reduction and effectiveness are approximately equivalent. At high doses (high prevalence or long exposure), the exponential relationship means a 50% dose reduction provides more than 50% effectiveness.
