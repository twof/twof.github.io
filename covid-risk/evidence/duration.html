COVID event risk calculator

Evidence and methodology for model inputs

Duration (hours)

Default value: 2.0 hours

How it affects the model

Duration enters the infection probability calculation in two ways:

  1. Cumulative dose. The inhaled dose of infectious quanta scales with time: dose = concentration × breathing_rate × duration. Longer events mean more total virus inhaled.
  2. Transient concentration buildup. When an infectious person enters a room, airborne virus concentration starts at zero and rises toward a steady state. The model applies a correction factor that accounts for this buildup: 1 − (1 − e−ACH·T) / (ACH · T), where ACH is air changes per hour and T is the duration. For a 2-hour event at ACH=3, this factor is ~0.83, meaning the actual dose is 83% of what a steady-state assumption would predict. For short events, the correction is larger (more time spent at below-steady-state concentrations).

The infection probability follows the exponential dose-response: P = 1 − e−dose. At the low per-person probabilities this model typically produces, risk scales approximately linearly with duration. At higher probabilities, it saturates toward 100%.

Evidence

The exponential dose-response is validated for human coronavirus

Aganovic & Kadric (2024) compared four dose-response models (exponential, Stirling approximated β-Poisson, exact β-Poisson, and Laplace approximated β-Poisson) against human challenge data for HCoV-229E (a human coronavirus) and two human rhinoviruses. For the coronavirus dataset, the exponential model — the same P = 1 − e−kd used in the Wells-Riley framework and in this calculator — was the best fitting model (k = 0.054, AIC = 37.64, rank 1 of 4).

Notably, the exponential model was rejected for the pooled rhinovirus dataset, where the Laplace β-Poisson fit better. This means the exponential dose-response is not universal across respiratory viruses — it matters that it was validated for a coronavirus specifically.

A 2026 correction to the paper clarified the interpretation of the Laplace approximation method but confirmed all numerical results and infection risk estimates are unchanged.

Caveat: HCoV-229E is a common-cold coronavirus, not SARS-CoV-2. The only human challenge study for SARS-CoV-2 (Killingley et al. 2022) used intranasal inoculation, not aerosol exposure, so it does not directly validate the airborne dose-response relationship.

Measured SARS-CoV-2 emission rates produce plausible time-to-infection

Alsved et al. (2023) performed the first quantification of infectious SARS-CoV-2 emission rates from exhaled aerosols, measuring TCID50 directly from the exhaled breath of three infected individuals. Emission rates during singing ranged from 4 to 127 TCID50/s; during talking, 3–4 TCID50/s; during breathing, below detection.

Using these measured rates in a mass-balance indoor air model (room 4×4×3 m, inhalation rate 0.54 m³/h), they estimated time to inhale one infectious dose (ID50 = 10 TCID50 from the Killingley human challenge study):

  • Transient scenario (infectious person just arrived): 6–47 min depending on the individual’s emission rate
  • Steady-state scenario (infectious person present for hours): 1–57 min

The transient buildup model used in the paper is the same mass-balance equation that this calculator’s transientDoseFactor implements. The inhalation rate of 0.54 m³/h is consistent with our default of 0.5 m³/h.

Duration curves in a comprehensive Wells-Riley model

Aganovic et al. (2023) developed an advanced Wells-Riley model incorporating nine removal mechanisms and variant-specific quanta emission rates. Their Figure 4 shows infection probability as a function of exposure time (0–360 min) for a classroom scenario (64 m², 3 m height, 1 infected + 20 susceptible). Representative values for speaking with the Omicron variant at 0.5 ACH:

  • 60 min: ~3%
  • 120 min: ~15%
  • 180 min: ~23%
  • 360 min: ~40%

These curves follow the 1 − e−kt shape that our model produces. The paper also confirmed a breathing rate of 0.52 m³/h for resting and standing.

The same paper found that quanta emission rates in previous studies were overestimated by up to 10× due to reliance on SARS-CoV-1 data and assumptions, and derived revised values for SARS-CoV-2: 0.13 quanta/h for breathing and 3.8 quanta/h for speaking (original strain), with an Omicron variant multiplier of 11.7×.

Limitations

  • Removal mechanisms. Our transient dose factor uses only ventilation (ACH) as the removal term. The Aganovic et al. model includes nine terms (ventilation, deposition, viral inactivation, respiratory absorption, UVGI, portable air cleaners, recirculated air filtration, face mask filtration, and incomplete mixing). At ACH ≥ 3, ventilation dominates and the simplification is reasonable.
  • Well-mixed assumption. The model assumes uniform concentration throughout the room. At low ventilation rates (<2 ACH), a two-zone model shows the single-zone approach can underestimate risk by up to 15%. At higher ventilation rates (>6 ACH), the difference is <5%.
  • Continuous exposure. The model assumes all attendees are present for the full duration. In practice, people arrive and leave at different times, and may step outside during the event.
