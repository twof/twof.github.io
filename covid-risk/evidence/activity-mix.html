COVID event risk calculator

Evidence and methodology for model inputs

Activity mix

Default: 68% quiet breathing, 21% speaking, 3% singing/loud, 8% eating

How it affects the model

Different respiratory activities produce vastly different amounts of infectious aerosol. The model assigns each activity a quanta emission rate (infectious doses per hour) and computes a time-weighted average based on the activity mix. Activities that produce more aerosol (singing, loud speech) contribute disproportionately to infection risk even if they occupy a small fraction of event time.

The model uses a lognormal distribution for quanta rates, characterized by a median and 90th percentile for each activity:

No additional calibration factor is applied. Previous versions of this model used an ad-hoc scaling factor to match observed infection rates; this was removed so that each parameter stands on its own evidence base.

Evidence

Aerosol emission increases dramatically with vocalization

The physical basis for activity-dependent emission is well-established. Vocalization generates aerosol particles through vibration of the vocal folds and turbulent airflow in the respiratory tract. Louder vocalization and singing produce substantially more particles than quiet breathing.

A quantitative microbial risk assessment (Mikszewski et al. 2022) measured expelled aerosol volumes over 20 minutes at 108 RNA copies/mL:

  • Breathing: 44 picoliters
  • Speaking: 260–360 picoliters (~7× breathing)
  • Singing: 4,300 picoliters (~100× breathing)
  • Coughing: 47–4,900 picoliters (highly variable)

Alsved et al. (2023) directly measured infectious SARS-CoV-2 emission from three individuals. Emission rates during singing ranged from 4 to 127 TCID50/s; during talking, 3–4 TCID50/s; during breathing, below detection. The singing:talking ratio ranged from 1.3× to 32× across individuals, reflecting enormous person-to-person variability.

Quanta emission rates vary widely across studies

The absolute quanta emission rates are highly uncertain, with roughly a 10× range across published estimates for any given activity:

Breathing0.72 q/h0.13 q/h0.4 q/h
Speaking9.7 q/h3.8 q/h8.0 q/h
Singing~62 q/h9.0 q/h64.0 q/h

Aganovic et al. (2023) found that previous estimates (including Buonanno 2020) were “overestimated even by factor 10 because of using data and assumptions being based on SARS-CoV-1.” Their revised values for the original SARS-CoV-2 strain are considerably lower.

However, Omicron variants have higher intrinsic transmissibility. Aganovic estimated a variant multiplier of 11.7× for Omicron, which would bring their rates back up (e.g., speaking: 44.5 q/h for Omicron vs 3.8 for original strain). The appropriate multiplier for current (2026) variants in a population with widespread hybrid immunity is uncertain.

Measurement methodology explains the 10× variation

Matvejeff & Tuhkuri (2025) systematically reviewed 77 respiratory particle measurement studies and found that methodological differences explain much of the variation in published emission rates. Among the reviewed studies:

  • Only 14% measured or calculated particle losses
  • Only 57% addressed sample dilution
  • Only 46% reported whether particles were measured dry or wet
  • Only 52% of vocalization studies measured sound pressure level

Their own controlled measurements (single professional singer, calibrated setup with loss correction) found speaking produced ~100× more particles than breathing by count, and singing produced ~10× more than speaking by mass — somewhat higher than our model’s singing:speaking ratio of 6.4×. However, this was sustained phonation by a trained singer, not typical event conversation.

Ratios between activities are more reliable than absolute rates

While absolute quanta rates span an order of magnitude across studies, the ratios between activities are more consistent:

  • Speaking : breathing ≈ 10–30×
  • Singing : speaking ≈ 2.5–10×
  • Singing : breathing ≈ 25–100×

Our model’s ratios (20×, 8×, 160×) are derived from the central estimates across these studies. Since the activity mix slider controls the proportions of time in each activity, the ratios matter more than the absolute rates for this parameter.

Loudness is a major factor within vocal activities

Moseley et al. (2024) measured exhaled aerosol and CO2 across activities in 15 participants and found that loudness dramatically affects emission within vocal activities:

  • Singing at 90–100 dBA produced 5× more particles than speaking at 70–80 dBA and 2.6× more than singing at 70–80 dBA
  • Vigorous exercise produced ~5× the particles of breathing at rest — comparable to conversational speaking
  • CO2 was correlated with particle emission for non-vocalization activities (r = 0.81) but poorly correlated during vocalization (r = 0.34), because vocal fold vibration produces particles through a different mechanism than breathing

This means the model’s single “singing/loud” category spans a wide range: quiet group singing at 70–80 dBA might warrant a lower proportion than loud projected singing at 90–100 dBA.

Superspreading events confirm the singing effect

The Skagit Valley Chorale outbreak (Miller et al. 2021) involved 53 infections among 61 choir members during a 2.5-hour rehearsal. Back-calculation from this event yielded an estimated emission rate of ~970 quanta/h during singing — far higher than any forward-modeled estimate, but consistent with a high-emitting individual in a prolonged singing event.

Limitations

  • Eating has no direct quanta data. The eating rate (2.0 median) is an assumption: mouth open, some conversation, but primarily chewing. There are no published quanta emission measurements specific to eating. The model separately accounts for mask removal during eating — during the eating fraction of the event, both source control and receptor mask protection are set to zero, so the eating quanta rate reflects only the physical aerosol emission of the activity.
  • Person-to-person variability is enormous. Alsved et al. found singing emission rates ranging from 4 to 127 TCID50/s across just three individuals — a 32× range. The model uses population-average quanta rates and cannot capture the impact of individual “super-emitters.”
  • Loudness is not modeled separately. Speaking at conversational volume produces far less aerosol than shouting or projecting. The model uses a single “speaking” category that represents a typical indoor event mix. A loud bar might warrant using a higher speaking proportion or the singing/loud category.
