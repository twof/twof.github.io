Evidence and methodology for model inputs

Mask protection and source control

How masks affect the model

Masks reduce transmission in two independent ways:

  1. Source control (exhalation) — reduces the amount of infectious aerosol emitted by an infected person. This is applied as a blended factor across all attendees based on the IC and non-IC mask mix distributions.
  2. Inhalation protection — reduces the dose received by the person wearing the mask. This is applied per-mask-type when computing individual infection probabilities.

The two sides have different efficiencies because of the physics: exhaled air is pushed outward through the filter material, while inhaled air can bypass the filter through face seal gaps. Source control is generally higher than inhalation protection for the same mask.

Evidence: inhalation protection

All inhalation values are based on untrained, casual wear — how masks perform when someone just puts them on without instruction. Face seal leakage, not filter material, dominates real-world inhalation performance.

Zhu et al. 2022 — N95 uninstructed baseline

Zhu et al. (2022) measured fitted filtration efficiency (FFE) on 21 participants donning N95 respirators (3M Aura 9205+) with escalating instruction levels:

Instruction levelFFE
Uninstructed baseline86.1%
Manufacturer written instructions93.3%
Video demonstration97.5%
Staff intervention98.3%

Prior N95 experience made no difference at baseline (p=0.95). The uninstructed 86% is the basis for our N95 casual value.

Duncan et al. 2020 — total inward leakage, DRDC Canada

Duncan et al. (2020) measured practical protection levels (PPL) via total inward leakage on 5–8 subjects per mask type. N95 subjects were trained and fit-tested; all other mask wearers were untrained:

MaskPPLProtectionTraining
N95 (3M 9210)16699.4%Fit-tested
Procedure mask, tie-strap3.672%Untrained
Procedure mask, ear-loop1.741%Untrained
Cloth (7 types)1.2–1.817–44%Untrained

Mean PPL for all 9 non-N95 face coverings was 1.95 (SD 0.89), roughly 49% protection. The study also cites that qualitatively fitting a KN95 to a headform gave only ~40% efficiency, rising to 96.7% when sealed with adhesive — showing fit dominates KN95 performance.

Sickbert-Bennett et al. (2021) measured total inward leakage protection factors (TILPF) on 11 trained volunteers:

These trained-volunteer values are significantly higher than untrained performance for all mask types except N95.

How inhalation values were chosen

  • Cloth (30%): Duncan untrained cloth PPL 1.2–1.8 (17–44%), geometric mean ~30%. Consistent with Clapp fit factor 2.1 (52%) being on the high side for a very small sample.
  • Surgical (40%): Duncan untrained ear-loop PPL 1.7 (41%). Consistent with the middle of the wide range across studies (4–72%).
  • KN95 typical (50%): Between Duncan’s cited 40% (headform, no seal) and Clapp’s 55% (fit factor 2.2, untrained). KN95s had 0% fit test pass rate.
  • N95 casual (86%): Directly from Zhu et al. uninstructed baseline FFE on 21 participants. N95 form factor provides substantially better seal than KN95 even without training.
  • N95 fit-tested (95%): Conservative relative to measured 99%+ across multiple studies (Duncan, Sickbert-Bennett, Zhu post-instruction).

Evidence: source control

Adenaiye et al. 2024 (eBioMedicine) — real COVID patients

This study measured exhaled viral load from 44 COVID-infected volunteers (60 paired masked/unmasked samples) using a Gesundheit-II bioaerosol collector. Participants received no fit training:

N95 (duckbill)98% (97–99)ACI 3120

Notable: KN95 performed worse than surgical as source control. The authors noted KN95s “did not seal consistently along the entire perimeter.” Only one model per category was tested.

How source control values were chosen

  • Cloth (50%): CDC manikin study ranged from 44–71% across cloth types. 50% is a conservative mid-range estimate.
  • Surgical (74%): Directly from Adenaiye et al. 2024 (real COVID patients, untrained).
  • KN95 typical (71%): Directly from Adenaiye et al. 2024. Slightly below surgical due to inconsistent sealing.
  • N95 casual and fit-tested (95%): Conservative estimate from 95–99% range across studies. N95 source control appears robust regardless of fit testing because exhaled air is forced through the filter material.

Default mask mix values

The IC and non-IC mask mix sliders control what fraction of attendees wear each mask type. These defaults are based on survey data about actual masking behavior, not aspirational targets.

IC mask mix (regimented default): 40% masked, 60% none

The KFF COVID-19 Vaccine Monitor found that 40% of immunocompromised adults still wear masks, while 17% have made no behavioral adjustments at all. The 40% who mask are distributed across types (5% N95 fit-tested, 5% N95 casual, 10% KN95, 10% surgical, 10% cloth). The type distribution is estimated — no survey breaks down IC masking by mask type.

Non-IC mask mix default: ~100% none

Pew Research Center (2024) found that 80% of Americans hardly ever or never wear masks, and only 4% mask frequently. The non-IC default of 100% none reflects this reality.

General IC preset: 20% masked, 80% none

The general preset represents IC individuals who are less health-engaged. If the regimented IC population masks at 40%, the less-engaged subset likely masks at roughly half that rate.

Limitations

  • Source control data is thin. The Adenaiye study tested only one model per mask category. The CDC manikin study found surgical mask source control ranging from 42% to 93% across models.
  • Cloth mask performance varies enormously. Material, layers, and construction matter more for cloth masks than for standardized products. The model uses a single value that cannot capture this range.
  • N95 source control gap is unmeasured. No study directly compared fit-tested vs unfitted N95 source control. The available data (95–99% in both conditions) suggests the gap is small, but this has not been rigorously established.
  • KN95 quality is inconsistent. Up to 60% of KN95s on the market do not meet certification criteria. The studies tested specific brands that may not represent the full range of products.
  • Behavioral factors are not captured. Mask touching, nose exposure, removal for eating/drinking, and improper donning all reduce real-world effectiveness below even the “untrained” lab measurements.
