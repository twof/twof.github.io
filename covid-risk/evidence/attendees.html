COVID event risk calculator

Evidence and methodology for model inputs

Attendees

Default value: 100

How it affects the model

Attendee count enters the model in two places:

  1. Expected infectious sources. The Wells-Riley equation computes the expected number of infectious people in the room as (attendees − 1) × P(attendee is infectious). More attendees means more likely sources of airborne virus, which increases the inhaled dose for every susceptible person. The −1 accounts for the susceptible individual themselves.
  2. Expected immunocompromised (IC) attendees. The number of IC attendees is attendees × age-weighted IC prevalence × self-selection factor. More attendees means more IC individuals at risk, which scales the aggregate expected infections, hospitalizations, and long-COVID outcomes.

At the low per-person probabilities this model operates at, both effects scale approximately linearly: doubling the attendee count roughly doubles the expected number of adverse outcomes.

Evidence

The relationship between group size and airborne infection risk is a direct consequence of the Wells-Riley model, the standard framework for indoor airborne transmission since Riley et al. (1978). The probability that at least one infectious person is present rises with group size, and the expected number of transmission events scales accordingly.

Empirical support for the group-size effect comes from documented COVID-19 outbreaks where larger gatherings produced more secondary infections:

  • Hamner et al. (2020) documented a choir rehearsal with 61 attendees where 53 were infected, demonstrating how a single infectious source in a large indoor group can produce high attack rates.
  • Leclerc et al. (2020) systematically reviewed SARS-CoV-2 transmission clusters and found that larger indoor gatherings (fitness classes, religious services, meat-packing plants) consistently produced larger clusters than smaller household contacts.

Limitations

The model assumes a well-mixed room: every attendee breathes the same average air. In practice, proximity to an infectious person matters enormously for short-range transmission. A 200-person event in a large hall may pose less risk than a 20-person event in a small room, which is why room volume and ventilation are separate parameters.
