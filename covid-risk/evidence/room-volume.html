COVID event risk calculator

Evidence and methodology for model inputs

Room volume (m³)

Default value: 10,000 m³

How it affects the model

Room volume appears in the steady-state quanta concentration:

C = q / (ACH × V)

Larger volume means lower airborne concentration for the same emission rate and ventilation rate. At the low infection probabilities this model typically produces, halving the room volume approximately doubles the risk.

However, volume does not act alone. The total air cleaning rate is Q = ACH × V (in m³/h). At steady state, the dose depends on Q, not on V independently. Volume and ACH are only separable in the transient phase (larger rooms take longer to reach steady-state concentration). For a 2-hour event at ACH = 3, the model is mostly at steady state, so what matters is the product ACH × V.

Evidence

Volume dilution validated against real COVID-19 outbreaks

Peng et al. (2022) analyzed documented COVID-19 outbreaks and defined a risk parameter H = re·rp·Ep0·fe·fi·D / L, where L = V·(ACH + λdep) / Nocc is the per-person air cleaning rate. Room volume enters through L — what determines risk is the ventilation-adjusted volume per occupant, not absolute volume.

Their Table 1 compiles outbreak data across a wide range of room volumes, showing that the Wells-Riley model fits observed attack rates:

Volume alone does not predict attack rate — the Skagit choir (810 m³, 13.5 m³/person) had 87% attack rate due to singing (high quanta emission) and long duration (2.5 h), while the slaughterhouse (3,000 m³, 158 m³/person) had only 26%. The risk parameter H, which combines volume with ventilation, duration, activity, and occupancy, successfully predicts the attack rate pattern across all outbreaks (their Figure 1).

Per-person ventilation rate is the key metric

The same paper establishes that the practical risk indicator is Hr = D·Nocc / (V·(ACH + λdep)), which captures duration, occupancy, and per-person ventilation. Hr < 0.01 generally avoids major outbreaks; Hr > 0.1 corresponds to high attack rates.

This is consistent with Aganovic et al. (2023), whose advanced Wells-Riley model uses the same C = S / (V × Σλ) steady-state relationship. Their Figure 5a shows the interaction between ventilation rate and infection risk over time, confirming that the ventilation-adjusted volume (i.e., Q = ACH × V) is the controlling parameter.

Limitations

The well-mixed assumption breaks down in large spaces

The model assumes uniform quanta concentration throughout the room. REHVA guidance states this is reasonable for rooms up to ~300 m³ with ceilings up to 4 m. Our default of 10,000 m³ substantially exceeds this threshold — one reason the model now adds a separate near-field exposure term that does not depend on room volume.

Geisler et al. (2024) compared analytical Wells-Riley, experimental aerosol measurements (ATMoS), and CFD simulations across four theater venues with different ventilation strategies (displacement, mixing, natural, and hybrid). Key findings:

  • Spatial variation in per-seat infection risk spanned orders of magnitude within the same venue. In displacement-ventilated DVV (99 seats), seats behind the emitter had pronounced aerosol plumes while seats in front were “almost unaffected.” In naturally ventilated NVV (470 seats), contamination spread throughout the entire venue.
  • Ventilation type dominated room volume. Global risk of infection RCFD for Omicron pre-pandemic conditions: displacement = 0.35, mixing = 1.00, natural = 2.50+. The ventilation strategy mattered more than the room size.
  • The analytical well-mixed model disagreed with CFD substantially in some configurations. For NVV especially, the well-mixed model performed poorly because concentration never reached steady state during the 2h event due to low air change rates. The authors note that a steady-state correction factor (as proposed by Peng et al.) is needed for venues where steady-state concentration is not reached.
  • Practical recommendations varied by ventilation type: displacement ventilation venues could hold 3h events with face coverings; mixing ventilation venues needed surgical masks for 1h events and FFP2/N95 for 2–3h; naturally ventilated venues needed FFP2/N95 masks even for 1h events.

For large venues, the model’s volume-based dilution is a rough spatial average: some attendees will be at much higher risk (near the source) and others at much lower risk (far from the source) than the model predicts. The aggregate expected number of infections may still be reasonable, but the per-person risk estimate should be interpreted as a spatial average.

Large venues: volume helps but doesn’t eliminate risk

Siebler et al. (2023) assessed infection risk at the Stuttgart State Opera using spatially resolved SF6 trace gas measurements at individual seat positions combined with Monte Carlo simulation. The auditorium volume was 8,149 m³ with displacement ventilation at 46,000 m³/h (100% outside air) and 692 seats (~11.6 m³/person).

Even with this very large, well-ventilated space, the reference scenario (no masks, no testing) produced a mean of 10.6 new infections from ~3.8 infectious persons (R = 2.75). Maximum safety measures (FFP2 masks + rapid antigen test) reduced this to 0.25 infections (R = 0.32).

The study also found that off-seat areas (entrance hall, intermission spaces, restrooms, exits) contributed meaningfully to total infection risk despite being a small fraction of total event time. These areas have smaller volumes and higher densities than the main auditorium — a factor that single-room models like ours do not capture.

This model assumes mixing ventilation

The well-mixed Wells-Riley equation assumes that supply air mixes turbulently with room air, producing approximately uniform aerosol concentration throughout the space. This describes mixing ventilation — the most common type in commercial buildings, where air is blown from ceiling diffusers at high velocity.

Other ventilation types behave differently:

  • Displacement ventilation supplies cool air at low velocity near the floor; it rises as it warms and is extracted at the ceiling. This creates vertical stratification (cleaner air in the breathing zone, contaminated air above) that the well-mixed model does not capture. Geisler et al. found displacement ventilation produced the lowest infection risk of all strategies — the model may overestimate risk for displacement-ventilated spaces.
  • Natural ventilation (open windows, no mechanical system) produces very little mixing. The well-mixed model substantially underestimated risk in the Geisler study (Ranalytical = 0.79 vs RCFD = 2.63) because concentration near the source was far higher than the room average.

If you are assessing a space with displacement or natural ventilation, this model’s results should be interpreted with caution and may need adjustment.

Volume per person vs. absolute volume

The outbreak data in Peng et al. shows that volume per person (m³/person) is more informative than absolute volume. A 3,000 m³ room with 19 people (158 m³/person) is very different from the same room with 300 people (10 m³/person). The model captures this correctly because volume and attendee count are separate inputs, but users should be aware that doubling the room size is equivalent to halving the occupancy from a dilution perspective.
