COVID event risk calculator

Evidence and methodology for model inputs

Annual non-COVID hospitalization baseline

This page sources the comparison table at the bottom of the dashboard. The table answers a different question from the rest of the calculator: not “how much COVID hospitalization risk does this event add?” but “what is this person’s non-COVID annual hospitalization risk for context?” The numbers are not used as model inputs — they are reference data so users can compare COVID-driven risk against background risk for their group.

What “unplanned hospitalization” means here

Reported rates are all-cause unplanned annual admissions: acute illness, infection, MI, stroke, trauma, exacerbations of chronic disease. Excluded: scheduled surgeries, elective procedures, planned chemotherapy infusions, outpatient dialysis sessions, and childbirth/labor & delivery.

Excluding childbirth matters: it accounts for ~11% of all US hospital stays and dominates the female 18–44 admission rate, inflating it 2–3× vs. males if included (AHRQ Statistical Brief #11). Since the calculator’s purpose is acute-illness risk comparison, maternal stays are not relevant.

The Healthcare Cost & Utilization Project (HCUP) Nationwide Inpatient Sample is the largest publicly available US inpatient database. Its statistical briefs explicitly exclude maternal and neonatal stays from age-stratified rates. The clean source for the 65+ split is HCUP Statistical Brief #103 (Hospital Utilization Among Oldest Adults, 2008), which reports both admission rates and the elective fraction by age, allowing the unplanned rate to be backed out by subtraction.

The CDC’s National Health Interview Survey reports a 5.9% age-adjusted rate of “any hospital stay in past 12 months” for ages 1–64 (2019), which is broadly consistent with the NIS numbers above when pooled across the 18–64 range and accounting for the planned-stay fraction.

IC adults by severity tier

Severity tier definitions match those used elsewhere in the calculator (hosp-given-infection evidence): severe = active SOT/HCT/B-cell-depleting therapy / advanced malignancy; moderate = biologics / chemo / dialysis; mild = controlled HIV (CD4 >500), low-dose immunosuppressants, asplenia, etc.

Severe IC sources

  • Post–kidney transplant hospitalizations and long-term outcomes (multicenter prospective cohort): median 2 rehospitalizations per recipient over 5.8 y, ~0.34 admissions per patient-year (~34%/yr).
  • Rehospitalization in the first year post-kidney transplant: 62.5% rehospitalized at least once in year 1; primary causes were infection and renal dysfunction.
  • Factors associated with unplanned 30-day readmissions after HCT (JAMA Network Open): explicitly unplanned admissions; 51% had ≥1 unscheduled readmission during follow-up.
  • USRDS 2024 Annual Data Report: 1.58 admissions per person-year for ESKD on dialysis (2018), declining from 1.82 PPY in 2009.

Moderate IC sources

  • TNF-α antagonist use and hospitalization for infection in Veterans with RA: hospitalization-for-infection rate 3.32 per 100 PY on anti-TNF (infection-only; all-cause unplanned would be higher).
  • British Society for Rheumatology Biologics Register (Galloway et al. 2011): serious infection rate ~4–5 per 100 PY in working-age, 14.2 per 100 PY in patients ≥65.
  • Hospitalization risk during chemotherapy for advanced cancer (Brooks et al., JCO CCI 2019): 6-month unplanned hospitalization rates of 20–50% by risk stratum — annualizes to 40–80%/yr while actively on chemo (a transient state).

Mild IC sources

  • Hospitalization rates among PWH in the US/Canada, 2005–2015 (NA-ACCORD, Davy-Mendez et al.): all-cause hospitalization fell from 22.3 / 100 PY (2005) to 13.0 / 100 PY (2015); patients with CD4 ≥500 and viral suppression had the lowest rates.
  • Hospitalization rates among PLWH, southeastern US, 1996–2016: well-controlled (CD4 >500, suppressed) sat at ~10–15 / 100 PY; CD4 <200 had IRR 3.32.
  • INFORM (Quint et al. 2023, n=12M, England): solid tumors (>5 y out) and high-dose corticosteroids both have aIRR ~1.4× population baseline → ~14%/yr applied to ~10%/yr adult baseline.
  • Bacterial infections following splenectomy / asplenia cohorts: first severe infection requiring hospitalization at 7.0 per 100 PY overall, 11–14 / 100 PY in adults ≥60.

Caveats

  • NIS counts stays, not unique people. A patient hospitalized 3×/year is counted 3 times. This slightly overstates the per-person annual probability, especially in older bands where readmissions are common (~10–15% inflation at 65+). The NHIS self-report figure (5.9% age-adjusted, 1–64) gives the per-person probability and tends to run lower.
  • Use 2018–2019 data as baseline. NIS volumes dropped sharply during COVID (Birkmeyer et al., Health Affairs 2020: −43% nadir in April 2020). 2020–2022 data underestimates the steady-state baseline, particularly for elective and to a lesser extent unplanned care (deferred ED visits). Rates have largely recovered by 2023 but remain ~5–10% below pre-pandemic for some age groups.
  • Transplant first-year rates are not steady state. SOT admission rates in year 1 are roughly 2–3× year 2+, driven by surgical complications, rejection, and CMV. A user who is a stable 5+ year-out transplant recipient should mentally use the steady-state number (~25–30%/yr), not the headline first-year rate.
  • Active chemo is a transient state. The 40–80%/yr unplanned rate during chemo applies for ~3–6 months; survivorship rates drop back near baseline within 1–2 years of completion. The moderate-IC tier will have wide spread depending on what fraction of users are currently on chemo vs. on maintenance therapy.
  • HIV literature is dated. The strongest stratified data is 2005–2015. Modern ART cohorts with CD4 >500 and U=U almost certainly hospitalize at near-general-population rates today; the 10–15%/yr estimate may be slightly conservative for 2026.
  • The general-adult baseline (~8–12%) already includes some IC patients, so “mild IC” sitting only marginally above baseline is mathematically expected and consistent with INFORM’s 1.4× aIRR.
  • HCUP Statistical Brief #103 is from 2008. It is the only source with a clean 65–74 / 75–84 / 85+ split AND elective fraction. Rates have declined since (NIS 2000–2015 trend: −25% in adults 65+), so the modern 65–74 unplanned rate is likely closer to 15–18% than 19%. The dashboard figure (18–20%) splits the difference.
  • No single source covers all three IC tiers. Triangulation is unavoidable; the recommended numbers are population-weighted estimates rather than single citable rates.

Why this matters for the calculator

The dashboard’s “Annual totals” section reports COVID-attributable hospitalization risk under a chosen events-per-year frequency. To interpret that number, the user needs to know what their non-COVID annual hospitalization risk looks like for context. A 65-year-old with severe IC sees a baseline of ~35%/yr; a healthy 25-year-old sees ~3%/yr. A COVID-attributable annual risk of ~1%/yr means very different things in those two contexts.
